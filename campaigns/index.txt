<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Share a Coke turned product packaging into a personalised invitation to connect. In Australia in 2011, Coca-Cola replaced its logo on bottles and cans with popular first names, making the pack itself a conversation starter and a potential gift.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The problem and insight</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In a 2014 account, Coca-Cola’s campaign team explained that research suggested younger Australians liked the brand’s iconic status but did not always feel it spoke to them at eye level. Personalising packs with first names was intended to make the brand feel more personal and to encourage sharing.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The creative platform</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Make the brand personal by putting people into the packaging.</strong> The execution changed the role of the bottle: it was no longer only a branded container, but also a personalised object people could search for, give to someone and share socially.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">How it travelled</h2>
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>Packaging:</strong> popular first names replaced the familiar logo on selected packs.</li><li><strong>Participation:</strong> shoppers could look for names and later personalise cans through activations.</li><li><strong>Digital:</strong> the campaign included ways to share virtual Coke experiences and encouraged social conversation.</li><li><strong>Local adaptation:</strong> Coca-Cola described markets adapting the idea, for example using nicknames in China and other locally relevant executions.</li></ul>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Reported outcomes</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Coca-Cola’s 2014 retrospective reported that the Australian launch sold more than 250 million named bottles and cans in a country of just under 23 million people, and said the idea had reached more than 70 countries by that time. These are company-reported figures and should be read as such. The brand also announced a new generation of the campaign in March 2025.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What marketers can learn</h2>
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Personalisation can turn an ordinary product touchpoint into a participatory experience.</li><li>Packaging can function as owned media when it gives people a reason to notice, keep or share it.</li><li>A strong global platform still needs local adaptation: names, language and social norms differ by market.</li><li>Build operational safeguards into open participation, especially when public submissions or customisation are involved.</li><li>Separate company-reported campaign outcomes from independent evaluation.</li></ul>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>Editorial note: This case study summarises Coca-Cola’s own retrospective and campaign announcement. The reported sales and reach numbers are attributed to the company, not presented as independently audited causal proof.</em></p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Amul’s topical advertising is a useful example of how a consistent brand character and a recognisable voice can respond to events in the public conversation. Rather than treating every advertisement as a standalone product pitch, the format uses topical humour to connect the brand with moments people are already discussing.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What is the campaign known for?</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The Amul Girl became a recurring brand character associated with witty commentary on current events. An IUP case abstract describes the character as the face of Amul since 1966 and discusses how the mascot has appeared across media, including radio, television and social platforms. An ICMR case abstract focuses on Amul’s use of humour in topical hoardings and the role of outdoor advertising in brand recall.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The marketing idea</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Consistency creates recognition; topicality creates a reason to pay attention.</strong> The familiar character and tone provide continuity, while the changing reference gives each execution a timely hook. The brand can participate in a cultural conversation without rebuilding its identity for every moment.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Strategy breakdown</h2>
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li><strong>Brand asset:</strong> a recognisable mascot and a distinctive, witty voice.</li><li><strong>Creative mechanism:</strong> link a current event or cultural reference to a short, memorable line and visual.</li><li><strong>Media role:</strong> outdoor placements made the work visible in everyday public spaces; later digital channels offered additional ways to circulate and discuss the content.</li><li><strong>Brand effect to examine:</strong> familiarity, salience and a sense of cultural participation — not just immediate product persuasion.</li></ul>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Why it matters for Indian marketing</h2>
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Topical advertising is especially interesting in a diverse media culture where humour, language and public references can travel quickly. But a reference that resonates with one audience may not work for another. Teams need editorial judgement, cultural sensitivity, timing and a clear link to the brand’s voice.</p>
<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What marketers can learn</h2>
<ul class="wp-block-list"><li>Build distinctive brand assets before relying on topical content.</li><li>Use cultural relevance to strengthen a brand idea, not as a substitute for one.</li><li>Keep the tone recognisable even as the subject changes.</li><li>Check context, rights, accuracy and possible sensitivities before publishing a topical execution.</li><li>Evaluate long-term brand effects separately from short-term likes or shares.</li></ul>
